# Verified references: NN clustering study, Cascadia 2010-2016

Checked against the Crossref REST API (api.crossref.org/works/<DOI>) on 2026-11-22. Title, authors, journal, year, volume, issue and pages below come from the Crossref record. "Key finding" combines the Crossref abstract (when there is one) with prior knowledge. Text marked [unsure] is from memory and should be checked against the paper.

## Required list

1. **ZBZ13a**. Zaliapin, I., & Ben-Zion, Y. (2013). Earthquake clusters in southern California I: Identification and stability. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 118(6), 2847-2864. DOI: 10.1002/jgrb.50179. **VERIFIED via Crossref.**
   Key finding: Applies the Baiesi-Paczuski/Zaliapin nearest-neighbor proximity (space-time-magnitude) to the southern California relocated catalog. The rescaled time-distance (T,R) distribution is bimodal: a background mode consistent with a stationary Poisson process and a clustered mode. Cluster identification is stable under changes in magnitude cutoff, catalog errors and parameter choices (fractal dimension d_f, b-value).

2. **ZBZ13b**. Zaliapin, I., & Ben-Zion, Y. (2013). Earthquake clusters in southern California II: Classification and relation to physical properties of the crust. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 118(6), 2865-2877. DOI: 10.1002/jgrb.50178. **VERIFIED via Crossref.**
   Key finding: Clusters fall into three types: burst-like (many small events, few foreshocks, short duration, typical of tectonic mainshock-aftershock sequences), swarm-like (more foreshocks, longer duration, lower productivity) and singles. Swarm-like clustering dominates in regions of high heat flow and low effective viscosity (e.g., Salton Trough, Coso). Burst-like clustering dominates in colder, stiffer crust. This is the main reference for linking cluster type to crustal thermal state, which applies directly to the ridge/volcano versus forearc/slab contrast.

3. **ZBZ16a**. Zaliapin, I., & Ben-Zion, Y. (2016). A global classification and characterization of earthquake clusters. *Geophys. J. Int.*, 207(1), 608-634. DOI: 10.1093/gji/ggw300. **VERIFIED via Crossref** (no abstract in the Crossref record).
   Key finding: Applies the NN method to the global catalog (M>=5 or so [unsure of exact cutoff]). Cluster properties (productivity, foreshock fraction, duration, burst vs swarm type) vary systematically with heat flow and tectonic setting. Mid-ocean ridges and other hot, extensional or oceanic settings show lower aftershock productivity and more swarm-like clusters. Subduction zones and colder continental settings show more productive burst-like sequences [unsure of exact regional breakdown].

4. **ZBZ16b**. Zaliapin, I., & Ben-Zion, Y. (2016). Discriminating characteristics of tectonic and human-induced seismicity. *Bull. Seismol. Soc. Am.*, 106(3), 846-859. DOI: 10.1785/0120150211. **VERIFIED via Crossref.** Note: this is BSSA, not JGR.
   Key finding: Compares NN cluster statistics for tectonic catalogs (California) with induced catalogs (geothermal fields, Oklahoma/Kansas wastewater injection). Induced seismicity has a weaker separation between background and clustered modes, more swarm-like clusters, fewer aftershocks and more foreshock-like activity, and its NN statistics look like those of natural seismicity in high-heat-flow regions. Usable as an analog for fluid-driven natural swarms (ridges, volcanoes).

5. **ZBZ20**. Zaliapin, I., & Ben-Zion, Y. (2020). Earthquake declustering using the nearest-neighbor approach in space-time-magnitude domain. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 125(4), e2018JB017120. DOI: 10.1029/2018JB017120. **VERIFIED via Crossref.**
   Key finding (from the abstract): Declustering by random thinning, with a space-varying threshold estimated from randomized-reshuffled catalogs that keep the spatial distribution but break space-time coupling. On ETAS synthetics it classifies more than 80% of background and clustered events correctly, and more than 75% of events get the same type in more than 90% of random realizations. The estimated background passes stationarity and space-time independence tests. This is the method citation for a declustered background.

6. **BP04**. Baiesi, M., & Paczuski, M. (2004). Scale-free networks of earthquakes and aftershocks. *Phys. Rev. E*, 69(6), 066106. DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevE.69.066106. **VERIFIED via Crossref.**
   Key finding: Defines the space-time-magnitude metric n_ij = t_ij * r_ij^d_f * 10^(-b m_i), which links each earthquake to its most likely "parent". The resulting network is scale-free, with no need to choose fixed aftershock windows. This is the origin of the NN proximity used by ZBZ.

7. **ZGKW08**. Zaliapin, I., Gabrielov, A., Keilis-Borok, V., & Wong, H. (2008). Clustering analysis of seismicity and aftershock identification. *Phys. Rev. Lett.*, 101(1), 018501. DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevLett.101.018501. **VERIFIED via Crossref.**
   Key finding: Splits the BP04 proximity into rescaled time T and rescaled distance R (log eta = log T + log R). The joint (T,R) distribution is bimodal, which gives an objective separation of clustered from background events. The background mode follows a stationary Poisson-like process.

8. **VS06**. Vidale, J. E., & Shearer, P. M. (2006). A survey of 71 earthquake bursts across southern California: Exploring the role of pore fluid pressure fluctuations and aseismic slip as drivers. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 111(B5), B05312 (Crossref lists the article number as 2005JB004034). DOI: 10.1029/2005JB004034. **VERIFIED via Crossref.** Note: the full title includes the subtitle.
   Key finding: Of 71 spatially compact bursts in relocated southern California seismicity, most are swarm-like: there is no dominant mainshock and they migrate or expand over hours to days. The paper attributes them to pore-fluid pressure fluctuations and/or aseismic slip rather than simple elastic aftershock triggering. Only a minority look like mainshock-aftershock sequences.

9. **RM09**. Roland, E., & McGuire, J. J. (2009). Earthquake swarms on transform faults. *Geophys. J. Int.*, 178(3), 1677-1690. DOI: 10.1111/j.1365-246X.2009.04214.x. **VERIFIED via Crossref.** (The DOI ending ...04374.x is a different paper, Ripperger et al. on Basel.)
   Key finding: Seismicity on oceanic transform faults (East Pacific Rise and others, with global and hydroacoustic data) is dominated by swarm-like sequences with low aftershock productivity and high foreshock rates. The spatio-temporal migration of these swarms is consistent with aseismic slip transients driving them [unsure of exact datasets]. This is the main reference for expecting swarm-type clustering on the Blanco and Mendocino transforms.

10. **KNB19**. Kuna, V. M., Nábělek, J. L., & Braunmiller, J. (2019). Mode of slip and crust-mantle interaction at oceanic transform faults. *Nature Geoscience*, 12(2), 138-142. DOI: 10.1038/s41561-018-0287-1. **VERIFIED via Crossref** (no abstract in the Crossref record).
   Key finding [from memory]: Uses a one-year OBS deployment on the Blanco transform (2012-2013). Seismicity on the Blanco Ridge segment extends below the crust into the upper mantle. Slip is partitioned between seismic patches and aseismically creeping sections, with the creep possibly tied to serpentinized or hydrated mantle. This supports mixed creep and swarm behavior on the Blanco.

11. **RLG23**. Ren, Y., Lange, D., & Grevemeyer, I. (2023). Seismotectonics of the Blanco Transform Fault System, Northeast Pacific: Evidence for an immature plate boundary. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 128(3), e2022JB026045. DOI: 10.1029/2022JB026045. **VERIFIED via Crossref.**
   Key finding (from the abstract): Uses 138 local OBS earthquakes and 84 focal mechanisms. The Blanco is highly segmented. In the west, seismicity concentrates in the deep pull-apart depressions (West/East Blanco, Surveyor). In the east, the Blanco Ridge is the most active segment. The Cascadia Depression is a short intra-transform spreading segment. The eastern part is a mature transform (about 0.6 Ma). The western part is immature, with stresses skewed clockwise from plate motion. Clustering may therefore differ by segment.

12. **LRG26**. Lange, D., Ren, Y., & Grevemeyer, I. (2026). Seismicity, repeating earthquakes, and tomographic imaging of the Blanco Transform Fault System, Northeast Pacific. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 131(5), e2025JB031584. DOI: 10.1029/2025JB031584. **VERIFIED via Crossref** (the paper exists; there is also an EGU26 abstract, 10.5194/egusphere-egu26-21326).
   Key finding (from the abstract): Uses 54 OBS for one year with machine-learning pickers, giving 12,708 events. Cross-correlation identifies bursts, most with interevent times under 5 min, and repeaters, mainly in the western Blanco and the Gorda Depression and fewer beneath the eastern Blanco Ridge. Repeater-derived slip rates are about 4 times the geological rate, so small patches fail roughly every 4 years. Coupling varies from fully locked to creeping. Vp/Vs above 2 indicates serpentinization. This directly supports short-interevent burst clusters on the Blanco. [unsure: whether the deployment is the 2012-2013 Blanco OBS array].

13. **W16**. Wilcock, W. S. D., Tolstoy, M., Waldhauser, F., Garcia, C., Tan, Y. J., Bohnenstiehl, D. R., Caplan-Auerbach, J., Dziak, R. P., Arnulf, A. F., & Mann, M. E. (2016). Seismic constraints on caldera dynamics from the 2015 Axial Seamount eruption. *Science*, 354(6318), 1395-1399. DOI: 10.1126/science.aah5563. **VERIFIED via Crossref.**
   Key finding: Before the April 2015 eruption, the OOI cabled array recorded steadily rising seismicity, with tidal modulation, on outward-dipping ring faults beneath the caldera rim. Seismicity peaked at more than 8,000 events/day at eruption onset and dropped sharply after the dike drained the magma reservoir. Inflation-driven ring-fault slip explains the long, swarm-like, non-mainshock clustering at Axial. Note that the 2010-2016 catalog spans the pre- and post-2011 eruption periods and the 2015 eruption.

14. **HMTBZ26**. Hsu, Y.-F. [initial unsure], Martínez-Garzón, P., Trugman, D. T., & Ben-Zion, Y. (2026). Earthquake clustering and stress inversion in the Mendocino Triple Junction region. *Geophys. J. Int.*, 245(3), ggag089. DOI: 10.1093/gji/ggag089. **VERIFIED via Crossref** (the title differs slightly from "Mendocino clustering").
   Key finding (from the abstract): Applies the NN approach plus focal-mechanism stress inversion in Voronoi cells. The MTJ has high background rates but relatively low aftershock productivity. Background intensity and aftershock productivity both decrease with distance from the MTJ (taken as the 1992 Mw 7.2 hypocenter), and so does compressive stress along the Mendocino transform and the Gorda slab. The northern San Andreas has a higher foreshock fraction, lower aftershock fraction and more swarm-like clusters, possibly from slab-window extension and high heat flow. Gorda slab mechanisms are mostly strike-slip and normal.

15. **SBC15 / SBC16**. Two Skoumal papers verified. Crossref did not return a dedicated western-US natural swarm catalog paper by Skoumal (UNVERIFIED that one exists).
   - Skoumal, R. J., Brudzinski, M. R., & Currie, B. S. (2015). Distinguishing induced seismicity from natural seismicity in Ohio: Demonstrating the utility of waveform template matching. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 120(9), 6284-6296. DOI: 10.1002/2015JB012265. **VERIFIED via Crossref.** Key finding [from memory]: Template matching greatly expands catalogs. Induced sequences tied to specific wells show swarm-like timing linked to operations, whereas natural Ohio seismicity looks different.
   - Skoumal, R. J., Brudzinski, M. R., & Currie, B. S. (2016). An efficient repeating signal detector to investigate earthquake swarms. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 121(8), 5880-5897. DOI: 10.1002/2016JB012981. **VERIFIED via Crossref.** Key finding (from the abstract): A single-station repeating-waveform detector based on agglomerative clustering finds as many or more swarm events as existing catalogs at Mammoth Mountain (volcanic), Oaxaca (subduction), and Alberta and Ohio (induced). Repetitive swarms are a common signature of fluid-driven and slow-slip processes. Probably the more relevant Skoumal paper for swarm interpretation.
   - Possible substitute (EGU abstract only, not peer reviewed): Pesicek & Prejean (2023), "A catalog of volcanic earthquake swarms in the United States", 10.5194/egusphere-egu23-10114. The Crossref record exists, but it is not a journal paper.

16. **Cascade volcanoes**
   - **ZGSTMT22**. Zhang, H., Glasgow, M., Schmandt, B., Thelen, W. A., Moran, S. C., & Thomas, A. M. (2022). Revisiting the depth distribution of seismicity before and after the 2004-2008 eruption of Mount St. Helens. *J. Volcanol. Geotherm. Res.*, 430, 107629. DOI: 10.1016/j.jvolgeores.2022.107629. **VERIFIED via Crossref.** Key finding [from memory, unsure of details]: Relocated MSH seismicity shows post-2008 earthquakes continuing in a columnar zone beneath the edifice down to about 10 km. The paper reads the deeper post-eruption seismicity as recharge or re-pressurization of the magma reservoir. This supports background-dominated low-level volcanic seismicity at MSH in 2010-2016.
   - **J05**. Jones, J., & Malone, S. D. (2005). Mount Hood earthquake activity: Volcanic or tectonic origins? *Bull. Seismol. Soc. Am.*, 95(3), 818-832. DOI: 10.1785/0120040019. **VERIFIED via Crossref** (Crossref lists only "Jones J"; Malone as co-author is from memory). Key finding [from memory]: Mount Hood seismicity consists mainly of recurrent shallow swarms south of the summit plus deeper events. Focal mechanisms and stress orientations match the regional tectonic stress more than a volcanic source, so the swarms are mostly tectonic with possible fluid involvement. This predates 2008. I did not find a post-2008 Mount Hood swarm paper through Crossref (UNVERIFIED).

## Additional verified papers

17. **MGZBZ18**. Martínez-Garzón, P., Zaliapin, I., Ben-Zion, Y., Kwiatek, G., & Bohnhoff, M. (2018). Comparative study of earthquake clustering in relation to hydraulic activities at geothermal fields in California. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 123(5), 4041-4062. DOI: 10.1029/2017JB014972. **VERIFIED.** NN cluster statistics at The Geysers, Coso and Salton Sea respond to net fluid balance. The fraction of singles drops significantly when fluid balance is high. This is a fluid-forcing analog for hydrothermal ridge and volcano settings.

18. **ZBZ22**. Zaliapin, I., & Ben-Zion, Y. (2022). Perspectives on clustering and declustering of earthquakes. *Seismol. Res. Lett.*, 93(1), 386-401. DOI: 10.1785/0220210127. **VERIFIED.** Short instrumental catalogs are dominated by the clustering of a few large events and may not represent long-term behavior. The paper proposes an ROC-based clustering measure that is free of marginal space and time inhomogeneities. Relevant caveat for a 7-year catalog that contains the 2011 and 2015 Axial eruptions.

19. **S12**. Shearer, P. M. (2012). Self-similar earthquake triggering, Båth's law, and foreshock/aftershock magnitudes: Simulations, theory, and results for southern California. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 117(B6), B06310 (Crossref article number 2011JB008957). DOI: 10.1029/2011JB008957. **VERIFIED.** Self-similar ETAS-type triggering has trouble matching both Båth's law (largest aftershock about 1.2 units below the mainshock) and the observed foreshock and aftershock magnitudes. The paper discusses how catalog incompleteness and declustering choices affect these statistics. See also the Hainzl (2013) comment, 10.1002/jgrb.50132.

20. **HS03**. Helmstetter, A., & Sornette, D. (2003). Båth's law derived from the Gutenberg-Richter law and from aftershock properties. *Geophys. Res. Lett.*, 30(20), 2069 (Crossref article number 2003GL018186). DOI: 10.1029/2003GL018186. **VERIFIED.** Båth's law follows from GR statistics plus ETAS-type cascading with productivity exponent alpha < b. Low-productivity (swarm-like) regimes break the standard 1.2 magnitude-unit gap.

21. **R85**. Reasenberg, P. (1985). Second-order moment of central California seismicity, 1969-1982. *J. Geophys. Res.*, 90(B7), 5479-5495. DOI: 10.1029/JB090iB07p05479. **VERIFIED.** The classic window/link-based declustering algorithm, useful as a comparison to NN declustering.

22. **H77**. Hill, D. P. (1977). A model for earthquake swarms. *J. Geophys. Res.*, 82(8), 1347-1352. DOI: 10.1029/JB082i008p01347. **VERIFIED.** Conceptual model in which swarms occur in a mesh of shear fractures linked by fluid- or magma-filled dikes and extension cracks. Explains swarms in volcanic and geothermal zones (relevant to Axial, Endeavour and the Cascade volcanoes).

23. **CS11**. Chen, X., & Shearer, P. M. (2011). Comprehensive analysis of earthquake source spectra and swarms in the Salton Trough, California. *J. Geophys. Res.*, 116(B9), B09309. DOI: 10.1029/2011JB008263. **VERIFIED.** Salton Trough swarms show migration rates and low stress drops consistent with aseismic-slip or fluid driving. This is the swarm-classification analog for hot, thin, extensional crust.

24. **B02**. Bohnenstiehl, D. R., Tolstoy, M., Dziak, R. P., Fox, C. G., & Smith, D. K. (2002). Aftershock sequences in the mid-ocean ridge environment: an analysis using hydroacoustic data. *Tectonophysics*, 354(1-2), 49-70. DOI: 10.1016/S0040-1951(02)00289-5. **VERIFIED.** Hydroacoustically recorded ridge-transform sequences follow Omori decay, but ridge-axis (magmatic) sequences are often swarm-like and have weak or non-Omori decay. Supports separating tectonic aftershock sequences from magmatic swarms on ridges.

25. **MBJ05**. McGuire, J. J., Boettcher, M. S., & Jordan, T. H. (2005). Foreshock sequences and short-term earthquake predictability on East Pacific Rise transform faults. *Nature*, 434(7032), 457-461. DOI: 10.1038/nature03377. **VERIFIED.** Oceanic transform faults have low aftershock productivity but foreshock rates much higher than ETAS predicts. This points to aseismic preslip, a pattern relevant to Blanco and Mendocino foreshock fractions.

26. **BM09**. Boettcher, M. S., & McGuire, J. J. (2009). Scaling relations for seismic cycles on mid-ocean ridge transform faults. *Geophys. Res. Lett.*, 36(21), L21301 (Crossref article number 2009GL040115). DOI: 10.1029/2009GL040115. **VERIFIED.** Global oceanic transform faults show high aseismic slip fractions, quasi-periodic largest events and few aftershocks.

27. **T06**. Tolstoy, M., et al. (2006). A sea-floor spreading event captured by seismometers. *Science*, 314(5807), 1920-1922. DOI: 10.1126/science.1133950. **VERIFIED.** OBS recordings of the 2005-2006 East Pacific Rise 9°50'N eruption show seismicity building for months before a diking event, then dropping sharply. A magmatic-swarm analog for Axial.

28. **H10**. Hooft, E. E. E., Patel, H., Wilcock, W. S. D., Becker, K., Butterfield, D., Davis, E., Dziak, R., Inderbitzen, K., Lilley, M., McGill, P., et al. (2010). A seismic swarm and regional hydrothermal and hydrologic perturbations: The northern Endeavour segment, February 2005. *Geochem. Geophys. Geosyst.*, 11(12), Q12015 (Crossref article number 2010GC003264). DOI: 10.1029/2010GC003264. **VERIFIED.** A 2005 Endeavour swarm, interpreted as tectonic or diking [unsure], perturbed hydrothermal vent temperatures and borehole pressures regionally. Links ridge swarms to fluid-pressure changes.

29. **S18**. Stone, I., Vidale, J. E., Han, S., & Roland, E. (2018). Catalog of offshore seismicity in Cascadia: Insights into the regional distribution of microseismicity and its relation to subduction processes. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 123(1), 641-652. DOI: 10.1002/2017JB014966. **VERIFIED.** Cascadia Initiative OBS catalog with 271 forearc events (M0.4-4.0) plus 440 in the Juan de Fuca plate. Seismicity is sparse off Vancouver Island and Washington and increases south of 46°N and toward the MTJ, following slab roughness and deformation. This is the direct predecessor offshore catalog.

30. **MBR23**. Morton, E. A., Bilek, S. L., & Rowe, C. A. (2023). Cascadia subduction zone fault heterogeneities from newly detected small magnitude earthquakes. *J. Geophys. Res. Solid Earth*, 128(6), e2023JB026607. DOI: 10.1029/2023JB026607. **VERIFIED.** Subspace detection on Cascadia Initiative onshore-offshore data gives 5,282 events, 4,096 of them new. The downdip edge of interplate seismicity matches the 20% geodetic locking contour. The paper maps possible asperities. See also Morton et al. (2018), *Geology*, 46(11), 943-946, 10.1130/G45354.1 (seamount subduction off central Oregon), also verified.
